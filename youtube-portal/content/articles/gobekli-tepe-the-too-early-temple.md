## The puzzle

Göbekli Tepe, in southeastern Turkey, has been dated to roughly 9500 BCE — older than Stonehenge by millennia, older than the invention of writing, the wheel, and (we thought) settled farming.

And yet it's monumental: massive carved stone pillars, arranged deliberately, decorated with animals. Someone organized a lot of labor to build something that wasn't a house and wasn't a granary.

## Why it's a problem for the standard timeline

The textbook order of operations was: farming first, then surplus, then cities, then the free time and organization to build monuments. Göbekli Tepe seems to run that backwards — the monument may have come *first*, possibly even pulling people together into the settled life that followed.

That doesn't break history. But it does mean the order isn't as fixed as it was taught, and that's worth sitting with.

## The questions we keep coming back to

- How much organization does a hunter-gatherer society actually need to build something like this?
- What was it *for* — and why was so much of it deliberately buried later?
- If we were this wrong about the order, what else in the deep-time timeline is softer than it looks?

## Where we land

Göbekli Tepe isn't proof of a lost super-civilization, and we won't pretend it is. It's something better: a well-dated, well-excavated reason to hold the standard timeline a little more loosely. Sources below — go read the excavation work yourself.
